The week NVIDIA reports without Jensen Huang

The Earnings Wheel · Tuesday, November 17, 2026 · Chuck Thornton

  • Disclaimer

I had most of this week's letter worked out by Wednesday. Then Thursday came, NVIDIA announced that Jensen Huang had resigned as CEO effective immediately, and I threw half of it out.

If you missed it, here's the short version. Huang resigned abruptly on November 12, 33 years after he started the company. The board named Colette Kress, NVIDIA's CFO since 2013, as interim CEO, and she's keeping the CFO job as well. Harvey Jones, who used to run Synopsys, became the board's first-ever chair. On Friday the board told CNBC it is still choosing a search firm and hasn't formally approached any candidates. On Monday, a person familiar with the matter told CNBC the board isn't considering bringing Huang back.

The stock took it about how you'd expect. It closed Thursday at $166.19, down 4.3%, after being down as much as 14% during the day, and it gave up another 2% or so on Friday. It had already been sliding before any of this. As of Thursday it was more than 30% below its October 6 high. Monday was worse. The Commerce Department moved to stop Chinese companies from renting NVIDIA's chips through data centers in other countries, and the stock fell another 4.1% to $156.14, its lowest close since July 2025.

So today is a strange one. NVIDIA reports its third quarter after the close tonight, right on the original schedule, and it will be Kress's first earnings report as the person in charge. She's also the one who wrote the guidance for this quarter back in August, as CFO. That's the report I care most about this week, and I'll get to it below. But there's plenty more on the calendar, so let's start there.

The calendar

A quick word for anyone newer to this. "Before open" means the company puts its numbers out before the market opens at 9:30 a.m. Eastern, so the stock reacts that same morning. "After close" means the numbers come out after the 4:00 p.m. bell. The first reaction shows up in after-hours trading, and you see the real move the next morning. EPS is earnings per share, and the estimate is the average of what Wall Street analysts think the company made per share in the quarter. It's an educated guess. Sometimes not that educated.

A couple of housekeeping notes. Williams-Sonoma moved its report to Thursday morning, and a lot of calendars still show Wednesday. Veeva Systems shows up on some lists for Thursday too, but Veeva says it reports December 3, so I left it off.

The big names

Tyson got the week going Monday morning, and its problem is still beef. The U.S. cattle herd is small and cattle cost a fortune. Tyson earned an adjusted $0.61 a share, a penny better than expected, but the beef business lost $171 million on an adjusted basis. Chicken carried the company again, though chicken profit fell from a year ago. For next year Tyson expects sales to drop 3.5% to 5.5% as it runs fewer beef plants. The stock still climbed Monday on signs that beef is starting to improve.

Home Depot reported this morning. Comparable sales rose 2.2%, better than the roughly 1.5% analysts were looking for, and adjusted earnings of $3.86 a share edged past the $3.84 estimate. Housing is still stuck, though. Customers made fewer trips and spent more on each one. Home Depot narrowed its full-year outlook to about 1.4% comparable sales growth, inside the old flat-to-2% range, and the stock was down about 2% before the open.

Disney reported the same morning, closing out Josh D'Amaro's first year as CEO. Adjusted earnings came in at $1.77 a share against $1.67 expected. Keep in mind that Disney's fiscal year had an extra week in it, and Disney says that 53rd week added about $490 million to segment operating income for the year, so not all of the jump is improvement. The parks set records, streaming revenue rose 19%, and D'Amaro stuck with double-digit earnings growth for fiscal 2027. Disney will also stop reporting streaming operating income on its own. The stock was still down about 2% before the open, partly because Disney expects segment operating income to grow about 8% next year, down from 12% this year.

Wednesday morning brings TJX and Lowe's. TJX guided comparable sales up 2% to 3%, and the off-price stores like T.J. Maxx and Marshalls have been about the steadiest thing in retail. Lowe's has been out shopping. It bought FBM and ADG, two building-supply businesses that sell to builders, and that hasn't been great timing with new home construction as slow as it is. Their full-year outlook calls for about $92 billion in sales with flat comps.

Thursday starts with Walmart. Walmart guided this quarter to $0.62 to $0.64 a share, so the $0.64 estimate sits right at the top of its own range. The stock isn't cheap, and when a stock is priced for good news, "good" sometimes isn't good enough. Williams-Sonoma reports the same morning. That one is all about margins. Tariffs and freight costs have been eating into them, and management said last quarter would be the peak for tariff costs. We'll find out.

My 10 stocks, ranked by expected move

First, what an expected move is. Options get more expensive heading into earnings, because everybody knows a big move might be coming. If you take those option prices and work backward, you get a rough idea of how far the market thinks the stock could swing after the report. It tells you the size of the move, not the direction. If a $100 stock has a ±10% expected move, the options are priced as if a move to somewhere around $90 or $110 wouldn't be a shock. Stocks blow right through it fairly often, and sometimes they barely move at all. Think of it as the market's best guess and nothing more.

These numbers are from EarningsWatcher as of November 13, and they'll keep shifting right up to each report.

La-Z-Boy (LZB) tops the list at ±12.6%, and it reports tonight after the close. A furniture company at number one surprises people, but La-Z-Boy is small (about $1.3 billion) and has been hard to predict. Last quarter it missed: $0.43 a share against $0.49 expected, with revenue down 3.4%. The stock is around $32.50, a long way from its 52-week high near $45. Management guided this quarter to $500 to $520 million in revenue. The company-owned stores have been doing fine. Wholesale and the Joybird brand are where the trouble is, and the housing market isn't helping either.

Gap (GAP) is next at ±12.3%, reporting Thursday after the close, and this one is really an Old Navy story. Old Navy is the biggest piece of the company, and its comparable sales fell 4% last quarter, worse than management expected, partly because store traffic dropped in June. Gap brought in a new Old Navy CEO, Michael Francis, who took over at the start of November. Meanwhile the Gap brand itself has been on a long winning streak. The full-year plan assumes Old Navy comps of flat to down 1%, which means the back half of the year has to be better than the first. Wells Fargo trimmed its price target on Friday.

Global-e (GLBE) is priced for ±12.0% and reports Wednesday before the open. If you've ever bought from a brand overseas and paid in dollars with the duties already worked out, there's a decent chance Global-e was doing that part behind the scenes. Last quarter revenue grew 39% to $299 million, and the business beat estimates across the board. The catch is that expectations are high. Guidance for this quarter is $308.5 to $315.5 million, analysts are sitting near the top of that range, and the stock is around $41, not far from its high. When everyone expects a beat, a plain beat sometimes isn't enough.

Palo Alto Networks (PANW) comes in at ±11.3% and reports Wednesday after the close. It's one of my top three, so I'll save it for below.

Keysight (KEYS) is at ±10.1% and reports Thursday after the close. Keysight makes the equipment other companies use to test their chips and network gear, and right now it has more orders than it can ship. Orders have topped $2 billion for three quarters running. The holdup is the supply chain, not demand. Even so, the stock is down about 12% since its last report. It guided to $3.34 to $3.40 a share. I'm more interested in the first look at fiscal 2027, because a one-time tariff benefit helped this year and won't be there next year.

Intuit (INTU), the TurboTax people, is at ±9.4% and also reports Thursday after the close. Fall is the quiet season for taxes, so this quarter is about QuickBooks and whether the small-business side keeps adding paying customers. Intuit guided to $2.44 to $2.48 a share, and the estimate is $2.47. Credit Karma grew 20% last year, and Intuit expects that to slow to 11% to 13% this year. That's worth keeping an eye on.

NVIDIA (NVDA) is only seventh on this list at ±9.2%, which I find a little surprising. TipRanks has it a bit higher, around 9.7%. It reports tonight after the close, and it's my top watch, so more below.

Target (TGT) is at ±8.7% and reports Wednesday before the open. That's my third pick, and I'll come back to it.

Ross Stores (ROST) is priced for ±7.2% and reports Thursday after the close. Ross had a terrific quarter last time, with double-digit sales growth led by home goods. Management guided this quarter's comparable sales up 6% to 7%, so it's expecting a slowdown. If Ross lands at the top of that range or beats it, the story that it's pulling in new shoppers holds up. If comps come in under 6%, people will start asking whether the run is over.

Alibaba (BABA) rounds out the ten at ±7.0%. It reports Thursday before the U.S. open, with its call at 7:00 a.m. Eastern. The excitement there is cloud, where analysts expect growth somewhere in the low-to-mid 50% range. The worry is how much Alibaba is spending on AI data centers to get that growth, and its free cash flow has gone negative. The stock has been sliding toward the bottom of its recent range going in.

My top three

NVIDIA

The quarter ended October 25, before any of last week's news, so I'd expect the numbers themselves to be fine. Back in August NVIDIA guided to $108 billion in revenue, plus or minus 2%, and that assumed no data-center chip sales to China. Analysts are at about $109 billion in revenue and $2.47 to $2.49 a share. What matters is what Kress says about the future. In August she said growth from the big cloud companies should pick up again in the fourth quarter as supply of the new Rubin chips ramps, and she gave an early estimate of about 70% revenue growth for next fiscal year. If she stands behind those numbers tonight, I think a lot of the fear comes out of the stock. If she softens them even a little, people will blame the change at the top, fair or not. Wall Street is split. Bernstein and Evercore both downgraded the stock after the news, while Wells Fargo called Huang's exit a possible "clearing event". She'll also get asked about Monday's Commerce rule, which now requires a license to ship advanced chips to Singapore, Malaysia, Thailand, Indonesia and Brazil. With the stock at $156 after Monday's close, a 9.2% expected move works out to about $14 either way. Given everything going on, I'd have guessed bigger.

Palo Alto Networks

The numbers here take a little unpacking. Palo Alto bought CyberArk for about $25 billion, paid mostly in stock, so revenue is growing more than 30% while earnings per share are barely growing. There are a lot more shares now, about 818 million against 668 million in mid-2025. The company guided to $3.30 to $3.31 billion in revenue and $0.96 to $0.98 a share, and the estimate is right in the middle at $0.97. The number I'll look at first is next-generation security ARR, the yearly value of its subscription business. Guidance is $9.54 to $9.56 billion, up 63%, though a lot of that jump comes from the acquisitions. There's also a wrinkle: last year included a large one-time boost from a single big AI customer, and that ends this quarter. So the real question is what growth looks like underneath. The stock is down almost 6% since the last report, which tells me people aren't fully buying the story yet. That's partly why it's priced for an 11% move.

Target

I like what Target has been doing. Under CEO Michael Fiddelke it has cut prices on more than 10,000 items, and shoppers are coming back. Comparable sales rose 3.8% last quarter, with traffic up 3.6%, after a couple of rough years. Target raised its full-year guidance to $9.90 to $10.90 a share, but about $1.65 of that is a one-time tariff refund, so don't read too much into the headline number. The estimate for this quarter is $2.05, and the "whisper" number (what traders informally expect) is higher at $2.17. That means an in-line quarter could disappoint. Bank of America is skeptical. It rates the stock Underperform with a $141 target, against about $158 when it wrote the note, mostly because those price cuts cost margin. Retailers like Target are my best read on how regular households are doing heading into the holidays.

One important note

Estimates aren't results. They're what analysts expect, and companies beat and miss them all the time. Report dates can move, too, as Williams-Sonoma and Veeva just showed. And a company can beat its estimates and still see its stock fall if the guidance for next quarter disappoints. Over the years I've learned the market usually cares more about what's coming than what already happened. This week, with NVIDIA, that's especially true.

Talk to you next Sunday.
