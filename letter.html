Last week, and what matters now

That was a rough week. The S&P 500 lost 8.6%, its worst week since April 2025. The Dow fell 8%, its worst since March 2020, and the Nasdaq dropped about 10%. Friday did the most damage. The S&P fell 3.7% to 6,910.39, the Nasdaq fell 5.3% to 22,617.73, and the Dow lost 1.9% to 48,147.33. By Friday's close both the S&P and the Dow were in correction territory, more than 10% below their highs. The VIX closed Friday at 35.89, up 26% for the day and its highest close since April 2025.

Nvidia was at the center of it. The company beat expectations on Tuesday night and raised its outlook, and the stock barely moved: it closed Wednesday at $150.96, down 2.9%, which was well inside the roughly 8% move the options had priced in. Then the headlines took over. On Friday Reuters reported that the SEC is looking at Nvidia's accounting, and the stock fell 14% to $125.53. It lost about 23% for the week. That's my lesson for the week. The implied move prices the earnings report. It doesn't price whatever news comes out after it. On Monday morning, before the open, Nvidia announced that its board had reinstated Jensen Huang as CEO, less than two weeks after he resigned, and the stock opened more than 30% above Friday's close. The same lesson works in both directions.

This is Thanksgiving week. U.S. stock and options markets are closed Thursday, Nov 26, and they close early Friday, Nov 27: noon Central for stocks and 12:15 p.m. for eligible index and ETF options. Nearly all of the week's economic data comes out Wednesday morning.

The Fed held rates at 3.75%–4.00% in October. CME FedWatch, as cited by Kiplinger, shows the odds of a quarter-point hike in December above 97%. A hot PCE number Wednesday would back that up.

What this means if you sell premium: A Nov 27 option has only three and a half trading days left, but it still loses value over Thursday's holiday. Some of that decay is usually priced in by Wednesday afternoon, so don't expect a free holiday gift. Trading will be thin on Friday, spreads tend to widen, and closing a position before noon could cost you more than usual. The bigger issue this week is the VIX near 36. Premiums are rich because the market is actually moving this much. Size every short put as if the stock could move the full implied move against you, and then some.

Setup ideas for education

These are examples for learning, not advice. Prices are mid quotes from Nasdaq's Friday, Nov 20 closing chain and will be different Monday. One contract covers 100 shares. Figures are before commissions.

1. Cash-secured put on Dell, below the expected range
Sell the DELL Nov 27 $230 put for about $6.53 (bid $6.40 / ask $6.65).

  • Cash set aside: $23,000. Premium: about $653, or 2.8% of the capital, for one holiday week.
  • Breakeven: $223.48, about 20% below Friday's $278.02 close. The $230 strike is about 17% below the close, just under the straddle's low end of about $232.
  • Main risk: Dell fell 11% on Friday before reporting. With chip and server stocks under heavy selling, a miss or weak guidance could take it through the strike. You'd then own 100 shares of a volatile stock.

2. Covered call on HP Inc. through earnings
Own 100 HPQ shares (Friday close $27.96) and sell the Nov 27 $30 call for about $0.60 (bid $0.48 / ask $0.71).

  • Premium: about $60, or 2.1% of the $2,796 share value.
  • Downside breakeven: $27.37. If the shares are called away at $30, you'd make about $2.64 a share, or 9.4%.
  • Main risk: The quote is wide, so your fill may come in under the mid. The premium only covers about 60 cents of a drop, and the options are pricing a move of about 10%.

3. The wheel keeps turning: Walmart, step two
Last week's WMT $128 put finished 5 cents in the money. If you were assigned, the example now owns 100 shares at an effective cost of $124.08. The next step is a covered call. Sell the WMT Nov 27 $130 call for about $1.30 (bid $1.22 / ask $1.37).

  • Premium: about $130, or 1.0% of the $12,795 share value. Walmart has no earnings this week, which is why the premium is smaller.
  • New downside breakeven: $122.79. If the shares are called at $130, the whole two-week cycle (put premium, call premium and the $2 gain on the shares) comes to about $7.22 a share, or 5.6% on the original $12,800.
  • Main risk: Walmart rose 1.5% on Friday while the market fell. If that safe-haven buying reverses, your cushion is the premium plus the $3.87 the shares are above your cost.

Nvidia is the honest loss. I said last week that the at-the-money strike was aggressive, and it was. The stock closed $11.54 below the strike, and the premium covered only $6.85 of that. If you had kept the assigned shares through Friday, you'd be down about $3,000 against the $155.65 breakeven. That's what earnings-week put selling can look like when the news keeps coming.

The Walmart pair worked, but by a hair. Walmart closed 5 cents under $128. Options that finish even a penny in the money are normally exercised automatically, but when the close sits right on the strike, you won't know for sure until Monday whether you were assigned.

Risk reminder

A cash-secured put means you have to buy the shares at the strike, even if the stock gaps far below it overnight. Nvidia showed that this week. A covered call caps your upside and leaves you with nearly all of the downside. In a market this volatile, sell fewer contracts than you think you can handle, keep cash for every put you sell, and remember that the market is shut Thursday and closes at noon Friday.
